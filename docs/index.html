2. What a price implies if it is assumed optimal: P* = c + 1/b

Demand D = a·e−bP, profit (P − c)·D, maximised at P = c + 1/b, so an observed price implies b = 1/(P − c). With c = 0 this is revenue maximisation. Curves are normalised to a peak of 1.

β is not identified here. Each figure has its own αᵢ and only one price–sales point, so any β fits the sales exactly once αᵢ is backed out; the model prices move with the β slider, not with the data. Identifying β needs price variation over time for the same figure, a randomised price test (section 6) or a credible instrument. The joint optimum is also why a single seller has no reason, under a common β, to price hot figures higher: popularity raises sales, not the markup. A hot-figure premium has to come from scarcity, which section 7 models.

4. Rolling clearance heuristic for inventory

Each day the price is reset to P = ln(a·Tleft/Qleft)/b, the price at which expected demand would exactly use up the remaining stock by the end of the horizon, floored at salvage value. This is a heuristic, not the optimal dynamic policy, which needs a value function over stock and time. Tight stock (Q < T·a/e) gives a price above 1/b; overstock gives a price below 1/b. 2 hot and 4 cold figures, each priced on its own.

b is 1/142 for hot and 1/86 for cold (section 2 with c = 0). Without noise (sales then follow expected demand, in fractional units), the rule keeps the ratio of stock to remaining days constant, so the price stays at the "constant-rule price" in the KPIs; turn the Poisson noise off to see it. Rising or falling paths come from random sales, whole-unit sales and the last few days, not from the rule itself. A price line ends when that figure sells out. The marker shows the first sell-out, after which the confirmed set could no longer be offered; demand here does not react to that, which section 7 handles.

5. Inferring seller profit: purchase discount to per-box profit by channel

Official price 129 per box (774 per set), seller price 108 per box, hidden figure 335 at probability 1/72. Cost c = 129 × purchase discount. Profit per box = revenue × (1 − platform fee) − shipping − c.

Break-even discount for opening boxes depends on both the discount and cold sell-through; for selling blind boxes at 108 it is (108 × (1 − fee) − shipping) / 129. When cold sell-through is below 100%, opening boxes to sell confirmed figures is worse than selling the boxes directly, which is why sellers open fewer boxes.

6. Finding the optimal price from a price test

Section 2 assumes the current price is optimal, which is circular. To test that assumption, you need independent demand evidence: sales at the current price and at a nearby test price. That gives the slope b = ln(D₀/D₁) / (P₁ − P₀). With Q units and T days left, profit over the horizon is H(P) = (P − c)·min(D(P)·T, Q), where D(P) = D₀·e−b(P−P₀) and c is the unit cost, or the salvage value if the cost is sunk. Without a stock limit H peaks at P = c + 1/b. Below the clearance price P₀ + ln(D₀T/Q)/b the stock sells out early and sales are capped at Q, so H rises with price there; the optimum is therefore the larger of the two. The price is also capped at a ceiling for willingness to pay, because 1/b blows up when the test barely moves sales. The sales numbers below are placeholders: replace them with your own test.

7. One decision: box opening, figure prices and set prices

Blind boxes couple supply. Opening a box turns one sealed box into one random figure (hidden with probability 1/72), the confirmed set uses one of each regular figure, and the sealed set uses six sealed boxes. So the seller's choices of how many boxes to open, what to charge for each figure and what to charge for each set are one inventory–pricing problem, with the same structure as network revenue management: a set is a multi-leg itinerary and opening a box adds random capacity [Gallego & van Ryzin 1997].

Demand: λ visitors a day each choose one of 10 options (sealed box, sealed set, confirmed set, 6 figures, hidden figure) or nothing, by multinomial logit with option-specific price sensitivity βj = ε / observed pricej, so every option has elasticity ε at its observed price. Utility at the observed price is κ, plus the hot-figure boost for the two hot figures, +0.5 for the sealed box and −1 for each set. Leftover stock is worth the salvage share of its observed price.

Planning model (fluid approximation): for each number of boxes to open, prices maximise expected revenue plus salvage subject to expected sales ≤ supply. The solution prices every option at its effective cost plus 1/βj plus a common term, where the effective cost is salvage plus the shadow price of each unit it uses. The shadow prices make the opening rule explicit: open another box while its expected shadow value (5/6 · 71/72 of a random regular figure plus 1/72 of a hidden one) exceeds the shadow value of keeping it sealed.

Testing the plan with random demand

Four policies on the same random visitors (20 runs each): Observed menu keeps today's prices and opens boxes whenever a regular figure is out of stock (up to 12 a day), the common reseller habit. Static plan opens the planned number of boxes on day 1 and fixes the planned prices. Re-solving re-plans every day from the stock actually left, opens whatever the new plan says to open, and resets all prices. Re-solving, gradual opening is the same but opens only the planned total ÷ days left each day, keeping boxes sealed for longer. Boxes are opened one at a time with independent random figures; real sealed sets often contain one of each, which would reduce the imbalance.
